# Ascend demo scripts

## Two-minute demo: Learn, simulate, collaborate

**Spoken script (about two minutes)**

> Most students learn algorithms without seeing the decisions behind real jobs. Ascend lets them step inside a company and learn by doing.
>
> Here, I've created a bank in Real world mode. This 3D office is our workplace, and I can explore five careers by stepping into different roles. The company keeps moving: customer issues and operational events appear while we work.
>
> Say I'm studying the Ford–Fulkerson algorithm. In class, it's a graph of paths and capacities. At this bank, payments move through services with limited capacity. Now the question is practical: how many payments per minute can reach settlement without overloading any route?
>
> I choose software engineering and ask Maya, my AI coworker, to teach me first. She explains the source, the destination, and bottlenecks in this bank's context. Then I request a task. Ascend gives me `maxFlow` starter code, and I write the augmenting-path logic myself. I can run four checks, including a case that needs residual capacity. If a check fails, I inspect it, revise my code, and get feedback on what I actually submitted.
>
> Now another student joins this same company with a company code. They take customer support, document a customer's problem, and post a brief to our shared team thread. I read it as an engineer and ask a clarifying question before proposing a fix. When a new simulated event appears, we can pause the company, claim the task, work through it, and resume together.
>
> The AI coworker teaches and reviews, but the students make the decisions. Ascend lets us test a career, practice technical skills, and build the human skills that make teamwork work: listening, asking for context, and explaining tradeoffs.

**Demo cues (not spoken)**

- Create a **Neighborhood bank** in **Collaborative** and **Real world** mode. Show the office and live company feed.
- Enter **Software engineer**, ask about **Ford–Fulkerson**, select **Teach me first**, then **Try the challenge**. The `maxFlow` exercise has four checks. The starter code intentionally needs student work.
- Have another student join using the company code. From **Customer support**, post a problem brief; respond from **Software engineer** with a clarification in the shared thread.
- Pause an event, claim its contextual task, and resume after review. The paused-event assignment is separate from the Ford–Fulkerson practice task.

## Engineering: From an algorithm to a real-world problem

**Spoken script**

> Imagine you just learned the Ford–Fulkerson algorithm. You can follow its steps on a homework problem, but you're still wondering: where would I ever use this?
>
> Picture a software company during a traffic spike. Requests need to move through services with limited capacity. If we model those services as a network, a maximum-flow algorithm can help us reason about how much traffic can get through and where the bottleneck is. Suddenly, the graph from class has a purpose.
>
> That's the kind of learning experience we're building with Ascend: bring in a concept or a lecture PDF, enter a realistic company scenario, learn with an AI coworker, implement your idea, run checks, and improve it from feedback. You don't just memorize an algorithm. You practice deciding when and how to apply it.
>
> In today's demo, I'll show that learning loop with a traffic spike and a request-batching exercise. I can pause the simulated company, ask my coworker to teach me, write code in the workspace, see which checks fail, and revise my answer.

**Demo notes (not spoken)**

- Choose **Software company** and **Real world** mode. The simulated engineering event is **“A busy queue drops useful context.”** Pause on that event and take the software-engineering task.
- The simulated traffic-spike event uses the `batchRequests` exercise. For a Ford?Fulkerson practice task, enter that topic in the engineering learning flow to open the separate `maxFlow` exercise.
- The indexed knowledge base includes [Google SRE: Handling Overload](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/). A text-based lecture PDF can add course-specific source material.
- Ford–Fulkerson fits a **simplified capacity-network model**. Do not describe it as a direct fix for a production outage.

## Marketing: From a framework to a customer conversation

**Spoken script**

> Imagine you just learned the 5 Ps of marketing in class. You can list them on a test, but what would you actually say to a customer?
>
> In Ascend, you enter a simulated restaurant preparing to launch a seasonal pickup menu. You can bring in your class notes, learn from sources in the knowledge base, and talk with a customer played by AI.
>
> Before you pitch the new menu, you have to discover what that customer needs. Is convenience more important than price? Which pickup options matter to them? Your AI coworker helps you prepare, then reviews what you actually said and lets you try again.
>
> That's the difference between remembering a marketing framework and practicing how to use it with a person.

**Demo notes (not spoken)**

- Choose **Restaurant**, then **Sales & marketing**, and enter **“discover customer needs for a seasonal pickup menu.”** Select **Teach me first**.
- Show the source cards, then the AI customer conversation and feedback. The knowledge base includes [The 5 Ps of marketing](https://business.gov.au/marketing-and-advertising/the-5-ps-of-marketing) and [Develop your marketing plan](https://business.gov.au/planning/business-plans/develop-your-marketing-plan).
- The current marketing exercise is a customer-discovery conversation, not a full campaign builder.

## Career exploration and collaboration

**Spoken script**

> Ascend also lets you explore what it feels like to be part of a company. As you move through this simulated office, new situations arise: a customer reports a problem, a product decision needs to be made, or engineering finds an issue. When a task interests you, you can pause the company, step into that role, and try to solve it yourself.
>
> You can invite a friend into the same company, too. One of you might work in customer support and write down what the customer is experiencing. The other can take the engineering role, read that brief, ask clarifying questions, and work on a solution. You’re looking at the same problem from different perspectives.
>
> That’s something you rarely get to practice in a classroom. Good collaboration takes more than dividing up the work. It means explaining what you know, asking what’s missing, and understanding why another team sees the problem differently. Ascend gives you a place to practice both the work and the handoff.
